## B2
{#y1936} 1936. The modern computer doesn't exist yet. [[C03]]
{#tape_appears} So Alan Turing, still in his twenties, imagines one on paper: an endless strip of tape, [[C01,C04]]
{#head_appears} a head that reads a symbol, writes one, and steps left or right, [[C01]]
{#rules_appear} and a short table of rules. [[C01]]
{#universal_turn} Then he proves something remarkable. Write another machine's rules onto the tape, [[C02]]
{#morph} and this machine can play that machine. Any machine. [[C02]]
{#today_devices} However powerful your phone is, it can't compute anything this paper machine couldn't. [[C05]]

## B3
{#war} Then war. He's brought into Britain's secret codebreaking operation. [[C06]]
{#bombe} A machine he designed helps break Germany's Enigma ciphers. [[C07]]
{#classified} All of it stays classified until twenty years after his death. [[C08]]

## B4
{#y1950} 1950. He publishes a paper that opens with one line: [[C09]]
{#quote_open} "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" [[C09]]
{#game} Rather than argue over what "thinking" means, he proposes a game. [[C10]]
{#chat} A judge, behind a wall, types to two players: one human, one machine. [[C10]]
{#cant_tell} If the judge can't tell which is which, on what grounds do we say the machine doesn't think? [[N]]
{#child_machine} He also suggests: instead of building an adult mind, build a child's, and teach it. [[C11]]
{#thats_me} That's roughly how I was made: not written line by line, but learned. [[N]]

## B5
